**Thermostable protein engineering** has successfully leveraged *disulfide bond engineering* and *helical (α-helix) insertions* to enhance protein stability, though challenges and best practices differ for each approach.

**Disulfide Bond Engineering**

- **Adding disulfide bonds** is a widely used strategy for increasing protein thermostability. Disulfide bonds can stabilize flexible or otherwise unstable regions by covalently linking specific residues, often leading to increased rigidity and resistance to thermal unfolding[2][4].
- The success of this method depends critically on the **location and structural context** of the introduced bond. For example, introducing a disulfide bond close to important structural or functional regions (such as near the catalytic center) can more significantly improve both thermal and kinetic stability[2].
- A study introducing the A211C-S214C disulfide into an enzyme increased its half-life at 60 °C by 2.6-fold over wild type[2]. The strategic placement (near the catalytic center) was more effective compared to other locations.
- **Design strategies** include:
    - Structure-based design: Modeling geometric compatibility and flexibility to select optimal cysteine pairs for cross-linking[4].
    - Sequence-based design: Taking cues from homologous (related) proteins known to contain stabilizing disulfide bonds[4].
- **Limitations** include the risk of compromising native activity or stability if bonds are placed inappropriately, and the complexity of ensuring correct bond formation in cellular expression systems (e.g., requiring specific E. coli strains or chaperones to enable correct disulfide formation in reducing environments)[2][4].

**Background and Rationale**
- **Thermostable proteins**—common in thermophiles—typically have a higher proportion of hydrophobic residues in their core, which promotes tight packing and thermal resistance[2][4].
- Surface-exposed residues in thermostable proteins are often more polar, while destabilizing polar residues buried in the core are selected against[2].

**Effect of Surface Hydrophobic-to-Polar Substitution**
- Replacing *surface hydrophobic* amino acids (e.g., Leu, Ile, Val, Phe) with *polar* ones (e.g., Ser, Thr, Asn, Gln), can **increase solubility** and may reduce undesirable aggregation. These effects are generally beneficial for protein stability under some conditions, particularly when surface hydrophobic patches are prone to aggregation in aqueous solution[2][4].
- Such substitutions do **not generally enhance thermostability** in the same way as increasing core hydrophobic packing. The major route to high thermostability in natural and engineered proteins is usually **increasing hydrophobicity in the protein core**, not on the surface[2][4].

- Excessive hydrophobic surface exposure can destabilize the protein at high temperature due to aggregation[2], so targeted hydrophobic-to-polar mutation at the surface is often used to improve stability and solubility for mesophilic proteins, or to "humanize" therapeutic proteins.
